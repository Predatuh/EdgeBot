The one rule

Kalshi prices a combo as the product of its legs, so a fairly priced parlay wins 1 ÷ payout of the time. A 3-leg ticket paying 11× and a 39-leg ticket paying 11× are the same 9% bet — the long one just crosses 39 spreads to get there.

Leg count is not the risk. The payout is. You pick what you want to win; the builder finds the highest win chance that still pays it.

There is no price floor, on purpose. Kalshi's spread is about 1¢ whatever a contract costs, so a cent on a 97¢ leg is 1% of its value and a cent on a 50¢ leg is 2% — but reaching a given payout out of 97¢ favourites takes far more of them, and you cross a spread every time. On a real board, 5× costs 27 chalk legs at −21% EV, or 8 mid-priced legs at about −0%. Same payout, same win chance. Above 5× the chalk route does not exist at all. So a 55¢ game earns its place whenever it buys payout more cheaply.

What still protects you is the book, not the price: a leg has to be trading with a tight spread to be used. And legs under 90¢ are docked by how much that price band has historically underperformed, so a coin flip has to be genuinely cheap to get in.

Reading the numbers

True is the de-vigged mid — every outcome in the game normalised to 100%. Ask is what you pay. Edge is the gap between them compounded across every leg. It is always negative; a shorter ticket is less negative.

Draw loses. Soccer and Test cricket price three outcomes, and a draw settles a win contract at zero — so the draw stays in the denominator. A match quoted 55¢ is worth about 40¢ once the draw counts. Those legs are badged.
